Decision first, tree second, dashboard last. That is the order most metric work fails in.

0.2Why bother: the number went up, the business got worse

Two ad budgets, same product, 40% margin. Illustrative figures, but the pattern is real.

Plan A£5k spendROAS 8.0 · Sales £40kProfit £11k
Plan B£12k spendROAS 5.0 · Sales £60kProfit £12k

Plan A wins on the number everyone quotes. Plan B wins on profit and sells half as much again. Nobody lied. The metric sat alone, with nothing beside it to show what it cost.

The job of a tree: put the outcome at the top and make every metric answer to it.

01Decide itBefore anyone draws a box
1.1Write the decision sentence

No decision, no tree. Write the sentence before you pick a single metric.

If you can't finish the sentence: nobody has agreed who decides. That is why everyone keeps asking for "a bit more analysis".

1.2Four rules for the tree

One outcome, honest branches, an owner on every leaf, and a neighbour for anything you push.

One outcome at the topIf you can't name one, you've got a dashboard, not a tree.
Honest branchesRevenue is customers × spend. Retention is influenced by service. Label which is which.
Owner and decisionEvery leaf gets one name and a "we would…" sentence.
A neighbourAnything you push needs a metric that shows what the push cost.

These are practitioner rules, drawn from how metric trees tend to behave in organisations. I'm not aware of controlled studies showing one structure beats another. Treat the checks as prompts for challenge, not a grade.
