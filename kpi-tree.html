    Why most KPI trees end up as wallpaper

    What is a KPI tree?

    A KPI tree breaks one outcome into the measures that drive it, level by level, until each bottom box is something a named team can change. A useful one also says what you'd do if each box moved. Without that, it's an org chart for numbers.

    The number went up. The business got worse.

    Plan AROAS 8.0£5k spend · £40k sales£11k profit
    Plan BROAS 5.0£12k spend · £60k sales£12k profit

    Illustrative figures, 40% margin. Plan A wins on the number everyone quotes. Plan B makes more money. Nothing on the dashboard showed what the higher ROAS cost.

    Six moves, in the order that works

    1. Write the decision first. "We need to decide whether to fix onboarding before buying more traffic, by March, judged on recurring revenue." No sentence, no tree.
    2. Put one outcome at the top. Two outcomes means two trees. Settle that argument before anyone draws a box.
    3. Split by arithmetic where you can. Revenue is customers × revenue per customer. That's true by definition, so it's a solid line.
    4. Mark beliefs as beliefs. "Faster fixes improve retention" is a hypothesis. Draw it dashed and go and test it.
    5. Give every bottom box an owner and a decision. One name, and a "we would…" sentence. A box nobody would act on is decoration.
    6. Pair anything you push with its catch. Push cost per click down and watch conversion. Push handle time down and watch repeat contacts.

    Questions people ask

    What's the difference between a KPI tree and a driver tree?

    People use the names interchangeably. Some keep "driver tree" for branches that are pure arithmetic and "KPI tree" for maps that also include influences you believe but haven't proven. Here, solid lines are arithmetic and dashed lines are beliefs, so you don't have to pick a name.

    How many levels and boxes should it have?

    Three or four levels and fewer than 25 boxes covers most decisions. Stop splitting when one team can change a box directly. A box with one child should be merged. A box with more than five needs grouping.

    Who should own each metric?

    One person or one team per bottom box. Shared ownership usually means nobody acts when the number moves.

    How often should we review it?

    Review the levers at the pace you can act on them, often weekly or monthly. Review the structure each quarter, or whenever strategy changes.

    What does the what-if actually calculate?

    Add current values to boxes joined by arithmetic and the tool works out the top number. It then nudges each bottom box by 10% and shows how far the outcome moves. Multiplied branches move the outcome one for one. Added branches move it by their share. Dashed "influence" lines are skipped, because a belief isn't a formula.

    Is my data stored anywhere?

    No. Your tree saves in this browser only. A shared link carries the tree inside the link itself.
