The Data Playbook.
Analyse it. Chart it. Land it.

A free, practical guide to analysing data, drawing it honestly and presenting it to people who have to decide something. No sign-up, no course to buy. Open a folder and take what helps.

0.1How this playbook works

Most data work fails long before the chart. Someone asks a vague question, the analysis answers a different one, and the slide ends up decorating a decision that was already made. This playbook follows that order of failure: analysis first, then the chart, then the room.

  • Folder 01 is about getting to an answer you can defend.
  • Folder 02 is about drawing it so people read what you meant.
  • Folder 03 is about the human bit: titles, structure and pushback.
  • Folder 04 holds the tools you can use today.

My test for everything here: would this change what someone does on Monday morning? If not, it's decoration.

0.2Who it's for, and the small print

Analysts, researchers, product people and managers who produce or consume charts at work. You don't need to code. You do need to be willing to be wrong in public, which is the real skill.

This is my practical view, drawn from working in insight and analytics and from the standard literature on data storytelling. It isn't peer-reviewed research and it doesn't speak for my employer. Where a rule of thumb has limits, I say so.

01Analyse it properlyBefore anyone sees a chart
1.1Start with the decision, not the dataset

Write one sentence before you open any data: "We need to decide whether to ___, by ___, and this analysis will change the answer if ___." If you can't finish it, you haven't got a question yet. You've got a curiosity, and curiosity is expensive when someone is paying for your week.

A good problem statement

  • Names a decision and a decision-maker.
  • Is specific, measurable and has a date.
  • Is narrow enough to answer, but not a fragment of a bigger problem.

Diagnosis: most vague briefs are a decision-rights problem. Nobody has agreed who decides, so everybody asks for "a bit more analysis".

1.2Break the problem into a driver tree

Take the outcome you care about and split it into parts that don't overlap. Revenue becomes customers times average spend. Customers becomes new plus retained minus lost. Keep going until each branch is something you can measure or test. Two to four levels is usually enough.

Then list a hypothesis against each branch. Test them. Keep the ones that survive in the story and bin the ones that don't. Your audience doesn't need a tour of every dead end.

Watch for: branches that quietly overlap. If the same customer sits in two boxes, your arithmetic will flatter you.

1.3Check the data before you trust it
  • Count it. Do the row totals match the source system or the finance number?
  • Look at the edges. Minimums, maximums, blanks, zeros and dates that sit in the future.
  • Check the definition. "Active customer" means something different in every team that uses it.
  • Check the history. Did the definition, system or collection method change during the period you're comparing?
  • Ask who produced it and why. Data is collected by people with incentives.

A five-minute reconciliation will save you from the worst moment in analytics: finding out in the meeting.

1.4Separate signal from noise

Every metric wobbles. Before you explain a move, ask how much it normally moves. Plot twelve or more periods, not two. A 3% dip that sits inside the usual range isn't a finding. It's Tuesday.

  • Compare like with like: same season, same length of period, same population.
  • Be wary of small samples. A 20% rise from five customers is five customers.
  • If you looked at forty cuts of the data, one of them will look interesting by chance. Say how many you looked at.
1.5Correlation, cause and what you can claim

Match your claim to your evidence. These are three different sentences and they are not interchangeable:

  1. "These move together." Observational data can support this.
  2. "This probably drives that." You need a plausible mechanism and a reason to rule out the obvious alternatives.
  3. "This caused that." You generally need a test, a good natural experiment or a very strong design.

Habit to build: before presenting, write down the two best alternative explanations and say what you did about them.

1.6Say how sure you are New

Executives don't need a number with five decimal places. They need to know whether to bet on it. Give a range, a confidence level in plain words, and the one thing that would change your mind.

WeakChurn will fall 4.2% next quarter.
BetterWe expect churn to fall by 2 to 5 points. We're fairly confident in the direction and less sure of the size, which depends on how many customers take the offer.
02Draw it honestlyCharts people read correctly
2.1Pick the right chart

Choose the chart by the question you're answering, not by what the software makes easy. Try the picker.

2.2Credibility killers

One bad chart makes people doubt the other twenty. These are the usual suspects:

  • Truncated bar axes. Bars encode length, so they must start at zero.
  • 3D effects. They distort the thing you're measuring.
  • Dual axes. You can make any two lines appear to correlate. Avoid them, or split into two charts.
  • Pie charts with eight slices. Nobody can rank them.
  • Rainbow palettes with no meaning. Colour should carry information.
  • Cherry-picked date ranges. Start where it suits you and someone will notice.
  • Unlabelled units. Pounds, thousands, percent or percentage points?
2.3Guide the eye

Start grey. Make everything the quiet colour, then use one strong colour, such as our pink, for the thing you want people to see. If everything is highlighted, nothing is.

  • Delete gridlines, borders and legends you don't need.
  • Label the lines and bars directly, so the eye doesn't ping-pong to a legend.
  • Use size, bold and position sparingly. Each one is a vote for attention.

Squint test: blur your eyes. If the one thing that matters isn't the first thing you see, redraw it.

2.4Give it context

A number alone means nothing. 62% satisfaction is good, bad or catastrophic depending on last year, the target and the competitor. Always add at least one reference: a prior period, a target, a benchmark or a range.

Annotate the chart itself. A short note on the line at the point where something changed ("price rise", "new billing system") does more than a paragraph beneath it.

2.5A case for tables

Charts show patterns. Tables let people find their own number. If the audience will look for "their" region or product, a table is the kinder choice.

  • Right-align numbers and use the same decimal places.
  • Keep rules light, or use none and rely on spacing.
  • Bold the row or column that matters. Don't colour everything.
2.6Small multiples New

When a line chart has more than four or five lines, split it. Draw one small chart per group, on the same axes, in a grid. The eye compares shapes quickly and you can drop the legend.

Rules: same scale on every panel, sensible ordering (by size or by pattern, not alphabet), and one highlight colour for the panel you're discussing.

2.7Colour that survives real eyes

Roughly one in twelve men has some form of colour vision deficiency, so never rely on red versus green alone. Use lightness differences, direct labels or shapes as a backup. Tap a swatch to copy its hex code.

03Land it with peopleWhere most analysis dies
3.1Write titles that say so what

A topic title tells people where to look. An insight title tells them what to think. Write the second kind.

TopicCustomer churn by tenure
InsightHalf of our churn happens in the first 90 days, so onboarding is the lever

Read only your slide titles, in order. They should tell the whole story on their own.

3.2Problem, evidence, implication, decision, action
  1. Problem. What's at stake, in one sentence the audience already agrees with.
  2. Evidence. The two or three findings that matter, each with its chart.
  3. Implication. What it means for customers, cost or revenue. Make the consequence explicit.
  4. Decision. The choice you're asking for, with your recommendation.
  5. Action. Who does what by when.

Lead with the answer, then give the reasoning. Senior people can always ask for more detail. They can't get back the five minutes you spent building to a reveal.

3.3Presenting under pressure
  • Know your one sentence. If you got cut to 30 seconds, what would you say?
  • Put your backup in an appendix, not in your head.
  • Pause after the key number. Silence reads as confidence.
  • "I don't know, I'll find out by Thursday" is a perfectly good answer. Then do it.
3.4Handling the sceptic

Challenge is a gift, even when it doesn't feel like one. Work out who is likely to object and why before you walk in. Sometimes the objection is about the data. Often it's about what the finding means for their budget or their team.

  • Name the limitation yourself, before they do.
  • Ask what evidence would change their mind. If the answer is "nothing", it's not a data conversation.
  • Agree the definitions and sources with them beforehand where you can.
3.5When you've lost the room

You'll see it: phones come out, questions go off topic. Stop and re-anchor. Say "let me step back: the decision we need today is..." and go straight to the recommendation. Cut the detail. You can always send the rest afterwards.
